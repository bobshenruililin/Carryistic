# The second cut

Two editors can look at the same work and notice different things. The collaboration earns its place if a third cut helps someone see a decision Carry made that either solo cut leaves obscure. Carry sees one film. The director keeps the comparisons in the edit room.

## Exchange one objection that can change the film

Each editor identifies a shot in the other’s proposal, the exact frame where the idea gets lost, and a replacement they can show in six seconds. Codex renders both solo versions with the same length, source access and audio level. The director chooses one disagreement worth testing, then makes a third cut incorporating a decision from each editor. Write down which decision each supplied and why the third cut needed both.

For example, the current film passes from a vinyl label to a gold-rimmed opening in the sculpture. A critic could argue that the shared circle is easy to see but the gold edge passes too quickly to register. Try holding the opening still for half a second before the recorded camera turn. Ask a viewer what replaced the record and which detail told them. This is an example objection from the current handoff. ## Judge the work on its audience

Choose the question before the edit. For an admissions reader, ask “Which decision did Carry make while moving between media? Where did you see it?” For a commissioning client, ask “What would you hire her to do, and what in the work makes you say that?” Give each fresh viewer one anonymously labeled version for their first response. Rotate the versions across viewers. A later comparison can use the reverse order.

Count answers that accurately describe a creative decision and identify a supporting frame. Record the counts and denominators for each audience. A gain over both solo cuts on this measure supports the joint cut for that audience. Mixed results belong in the director’s notes. If work time or finishing quality differs, give each solo edit an equivalent revision pass before attributing a gain to collaboration.

Show Carry the authored cut and ask “What stayed with you? What did we read differently from you?” Her own words can change the story even when other viewers recall it accurately.

## Why this experiment

Dow and colleagues paired 84 people designing web advertisements. Pairs who shared several roughs incorporated more ideas from their partners, and their resulting ads attracted more clicks per impression. These were human designers making ads. The practical borrowing here is to exchange competing roughs while revision is still cheap. [Dow et al., CHI 2011](https://hci.stanford.edu/publications/2011/PrototypingDynamics/PrototypingDynamics-CHI2011.pdf)

Doshi and Hauser gave some story writers access to AI-generated ideas. Their stories received higher creativity ratings on average and were more similar to other writers’ stories. That finding motivates an independent first look at Carry’s originals. [Doshi and Hauser, 2024](https://doi.org/10.1126/sciadv.adn5290)

Jiang and colleagues ranked instruction-following answers from 11 language models, then fused leading candidates. Their system improved on the individual models on that text benchmark. Zheng and colleagues documented order sensitivity in model judgments of assistant replies. These findings motivate keeping both solo baselines, concealing authorship during review and asking actual viewers what the film communicated. Whether the method helps this portfolio is an open empirical question. [Jiang et al., ACL 2023](https://aclanthology.org/2023.acl-long.792/) · [Zheng et al., NeurIPS 2023](https://papers.nips.cc/paper_files/paper/2023/hash/91f18a1287b398d378ef22505bf41832-Abstract-Datasets_and_Benchmarks.html)
