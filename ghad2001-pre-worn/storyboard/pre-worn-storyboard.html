The story in one breath

Park keeps things simple and rewards a story told well. So the deck has one object, one question and one answer, and it follows the five sections of the brief in order.

Thesis, said aloud in the first two minutes

Globalisation made this pair cheap and everywhere by spreading its making, wearing and discarding across borders. Its health costs land where its record stops.

The question the room carries

Who made the hole, and who paid for it?

Our judgement test, stated once: does the person who bears the health cost have a record of, or a say in, the decision that caused it? The course asks for a criterion, and this one follows from what we found.

The mode: read the wear pattern

Denim collectors read fades the way a doctor reads an X-ray. Whiskers at the hip, honeycombs behind the knee and a frayed hem are the record of one body. A factory-made fade is a record too, of someone else’s work. So we read our pair the same way: each feature points to a process, a place and the person exposed to it.

The method has an academic name, which goes in the report: an object biography (Kopytoff 1986), or “following the thing” (Marcus 1995; Cook 2004). Pietra Rivoli’s Travels of a T-Shirt in the Global Economy (2005) and Miller and Woodward’s Global Denim (2011) are the closest models.

Title

Pre-Worn is recommended. Levi’s sells this pair already worn, and the deck asks who did the wearing. Unravelled is the fallback. It is accurate, but it is also a word AI writing tools reach for.

Opening hook

In 2022 a pair of 1880s Levi’s found in a mine shaft sold for US$87,400. A miner’s real wear made them valuable. Its pocket reads “The only kind made by White Labor”. Our pair’s US listing says “Made in China”, and its Hong Kong page says nothing. Labels have always told buyers who made the clothes when the seller thought it would sell.

What Park will listen for

These are predictions taken from the revision guide built on his lectures and exam guidance. Each one becomes a specific move in the deck, so the marker hears his own frameworks applied to our jeans rather than repeated back.

01

Flows, named precisely

“What moves? Through which route? Who controls it? What conditions shape its effects? Who benefits or bears harm?”

Chapters 2, 3 and 4 open on a five-line flow card in exactly those words.

02

Diffusion: circulation is not effect

“A pathogen arriving does not guarantee an outbreak.”

A ban is not clean lungs (F12). Insurance is not covered costs (F15). Collection is not recycling (F26).

03

Mechanism over listing

Claim, mechanism, example, implication, plus a qualification. “Defend the arrows.”

Each headline is a claim and each image is its evidence. The speaker supplies the mechanism, and each chapter ends on one qualification.

04

Topics joined, not listed

The exam asks for four of seven topics connected by mechanisms.

The pair is a junction where six course flows cross: goods, people, a pathogen shock, ideas, pollution and forced labour (F27).

05

No mini-histories

“Avoid tangents, mini-histories and isolated case studies.”

History stays only where it explains a mechanism: quotas moved production, and a label told buyers who made the jeans. The etymology and the 1873 patent go to backup.

06

A stated criterion

“‘There are advantages and disadvantages’ is an opening observation.”

One test, said aloud in F04: does the person who bears the cost have a record of, or a say in, the decision?

07

Scientific myopia

The whole versus the part: “learning to define our problems widely enough.”

Levi’s life-cycle study counts water and carbon and has no row for lungs (F07). The laser solved the dust problem and created a capital problem (F12).

08

Costs sit in different accounts

Birnbaum: company profits, health-system spending, lost income and unpaid care are recorded separately.

The conclusion places each cost in the account where it lands: a worker’s lungs, Bangladesh’s clinics, Accra’s drains (F27).

09

His own fashion example

Brooks’ polyester shirt in the pollution lecture follows extraction, production, fibre pollution and disposal.

We test that example on cotton. 100% cotton still sheds indigo fibres that reach Arctic sediment (F21), and Brooks’ second-hand trade leads to Kantamanto (F25).

10

Keep the benefit, change who pays

Plan B, “Who pays for affordable consumption”, and “equity can help make collective action effective”.

We say why a boycott would hurt workers (F14). Every recommendation names a payer and a checker, and none relies on consumer guilt (F28).

Eight rules

  • The pair is the only hero. Each chapter opens through its knee hole.
  • The medium tells the audience how sure we are: photograph, line drawing or paint. A small red tab marks facts about this exact pair.
  • Ground colours are sampled from our photographed pair: waistband, thigh, knee, then the white weft. The deck fades the way the jeans do.
  • One sentence per slide, written as a claim. Sources sit in one mono line at the foot, like a care label.
  • Real places get real, credited photographs, graded to the palette and never altered. Generated images only for our own object and for transitions.
  • Motion only between chapters and only on our own images. A documentary photograph never moves.
  • Numbers appear as quantities you can see, such as 74 dots or squares sized by share, rather than as a giant digit on its own.
  • One type family in two widths plus a mono for records. No italic serif accents, no cream paper, no accent bars.

The draft, re-read

The current 31-page draft has real strengths, and most of its research survives. What it needs is a different surface, and the evidence put in front of the illustrations.

  • The look is the documented AI default. A slide-design guide names this exact combination as the tell of AI decks: warm cream canvas, an italic serif phrase on the end of titles, muted red accents.
  • 19 of 31 pages carry a generated watercolour, and several stand in for real places: the Aral Sea, Xintang, Rana Plaza, Kantamanto. A marker cannot tell the evidence from the illustration.
  • One headline formula repeats, a statement followed by an italic reversal (“The exposure ended. The disease didn’t.”), on 9 pages.
  • Nine pages lead with a giant number. Each works alone. Together they flatten the rhythm.
  • Rubric gaps. There is no breakdown of components, shipping is a single page with no health impact, targeting rests on one campaign, and the immediate health effects of wearing are missing.
  • Over-hedged. Footers repeat “not this pair”, but the brief tells us to extrapolate from the evidence. One evidence key can do this once.
  • What stays: the exact product code, the failed trace, the silicosis dots, Fahim and his four institutions, who line-dries, price measured in wages, and the who-pays, who-checks logic.

The bench

This is the bar for this project only. A slide that fails any line goes back. The four groups follow the rubric, then a test for not looking machine-made.

Brief and timing 15 marks

  • All five sections named on screen in the brief’s own words, in its order
  • Every “point to consider” in the brief answered on a named frame (table below)
  • Two timed full run-throughs between 19:00 and 20:30, and a written cut list
  • Every member speaks for at least two minutes. Handoffs scripted word for word.

Understanding, research, critical engagement 30 marks

  • Every number carries a source code and an evidence medium
  • Each chapter has a claim, mechanism, example and qualification
  • At least one course concept per chapter, explained through its mechanism
  • One counter-argument taken seriously (F14) and one company claim tested (F12, F19)
  • Each recommendation names who pays and who checks, and traces to a finding
  • A real pair inspected, with dated photographs

Presentation skills 15 marks

  • One sentence per slide, 20 words at most besides the source line
  • Readable from the back row: body 24 pt or larger, sources 14 pt, contrast at least 4.5:1
  • Speakers face the room and use cue cards of five lines or fewer
  • An outsider can repeat the thesis and the hole afterwards (Sunday test)
  • Each speaker owns two likely questions

Not machine-made the anti-slop test

  • No cream paper, italic serif accents, accent bars or labels above every block
  • The statement-then-reversal headline appears once at most
  • Three giant-number slides at most
  • No generated image of a real place, person or event
  • No faceless figures seen from behind standing in for people
  • No repeated disclaimers. The evidence key says it once.
  • None of: delve, tapestry, landscape, navigate, journey, unravel

One deliberate omission

The forced-labour case is Uzbekistan because it has an independently monitored ending (ILO, 2021 harvest) and a remedy we can learn from. Xinjiang cotton is the larger current story. Including it is a group decision, given where we are presenting. The storyboard leaves it out unless the group chooses otherwise.

Why the slides look like this

In a controlled study with 110 students, assertion–evidence slides (a sentence headline over visual evidence) produced better comprehension and fewer misconceptions than topic-and-bullet slides (Garner and Alley 2013). This is the format the storyboard uses, and the report can cite it.

The report: rebuilding Astra’s draft

Astra’s draft is careful about sources and honest about limits. But it was written before the work happened, so most of it describes plans. The rebuild is in the same Google Doc, with the draft kept in the version history.

  • Tense. “Proposed”, “not yet agreed” and “remains unconfirmed” appear throughout. The final narrates what happened.
  • Show reflexivity through decisions. The decision log supplies four or five short stories of what we believed, what changed it and who proposed the change.
  • Name the method: following the thing, as an object biography. It gives the research a recognised design.
  • Add primary research: inspect a real pair with dated photos, email Levi’s Hong Kong about origin and finishing method (log the date and any reply), photograph a street collection bin.
  • State AI use plainly: what the tools did, what we checked, and what they got wrong. This week, a search gave 2015 for the Water<Less release when it was March 2016, and mixed 2009 life-cycle figures into the 2015 ones.
  • Task sharing as a table: real names, sections, sources each person checked, rehearsal role.
  • Headings mirror the brief’s four bullets. Aim for 1,500 words, and ask whether the bibliography counts.
  • Bibliography: keep R01 to R32 and add the new sources from R33.
